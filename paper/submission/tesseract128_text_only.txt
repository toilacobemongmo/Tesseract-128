TITLE: Tesseract-128: A 128-Bit Symmetric Block Cipher Integrating 4D Hypercube Permutations and ARX Diffusion for Constrained Telemetry Networks
AUTHOR: Hao Gia Nguyen (Faculty of Information Security, Posts and Telecommunications Institute of Technology, Hanoi, Vietnam)
EMAIL: haong.b24at088@stu.ptit.edu.vn

ABSTRACT:
We propose Tesseract-128, a 128-bit symmetric block cipher designed for resource-constrained telemetry and embedded platforms that lack hardware AES acceleration (such as ARM Cortex-M microcontrollers, small RISC-V cores, and automotive control units without AES-NI). On architectures without dedicated cryptographic instructions, conventional software AES implementations present an acute trade-off: 4 KB precomputed lookup tables (T-tables) provide acceptable throughput but exhaust small embedded caches and expose timing vulnerabilities to cache-collision attacks, whereas table-free finite-field matrix multiplications incur severe computational penalties. Tesseract-128 eliminates this trade-off by replacing Galois field matrix operations with spatial permutations on a four-dimensional hypercube 0,1^4 and 32-bit Addition-Rotation-XOR (ARX) modular diffusion. The spatial layer applies discrete double-isoclinic rotations from the Lie group SO(4) across orthogonal 2D planes, requiring an ultra-compact table footprint of only 192 bytes (21 times smaller than AES T-tables), which fits entirely within a single cache line or on-chip scratchpad SRAM. Mixed-integer linear programming (MILP) models, with exact ARX carry expansion, yield lower bounds of at least 78 active S-boxes against differential cryptanalysis (P_diff <= 2^-468 < 2^-128) and 108 active S-boxes against linear cryptanalysis within the modeled round structure. Miss-in-the-middle searches find that impossible differential, zero-correlation, and integral distinguishers terminate by round 4 in the truncated word-level model, leaving a 4-round margin relative to that model. In scalar software execution without hardware extensions, Tesseract-128 delivers 20.20 cycles/byte (166.56 MB/s), outperforming software AES-128 by 23.0\

Symmetric block cipher, Non-AES-NI architectures, 4D hypercube, SO(4) isometry permutation, ARX diffusion, MILP cryptanalysis, Authenticated encryption (AEAD), CAN-FD telemetry.

SECTION: Introduction
==================================================

Symmetric block ciphers deployed in real-time industrial telemetry, automotive bus controllers (CAN-FD), and remote sensor networks must guarantee provable cryptographic security within tight memory and latency bounds . While server and desktop processors benefit from dedicated hardware instruction extensions (such as x86 AES-NI or ARM Cryptography Extensions) that evaluate AES rounds in sub-cycle hardware pipelines, the vast majority of deployed edge microcontrollers - including ARM Cortex-M0+/M3/M4, low-cost RISC-V cores, and legacy automotive Electronic Control Units (ECUs) - lack dedicated cryptographic coprocessors .

In the absence of hardware AES acceleration, existing symmetric ciphers impose difficult operational compromises:

- The Software AES Trilemma: Substitution-Permutation Networks (SPNs) such as AES rely on Maximum Distance Separable (MDS) matrices over GF(2^8) to achieve rapid wide-trail branch diffusion . When executed in software without AES-NI, implementations must choose between two unfavorable alternatives:

- Large lookup tables (T-tables): Precomputing the combined S-box and MDS matrix multiplication requires 4,096 bytes (4 KB) of lookup tables. In constrained microcontrollers with small SRAM or shared L1 data caches, this footprint causes continuous cache evictions and exposes execution time to cache-timing side-channel attacks .

- Table-free Galois field arithmetic: Computing the polynomial reduction and xtime multiplication dynamically eliminates cache-timing leakage but incurs massive latency penalties, commonly requiring 50 to 100+ clock cycles per byte on 32-bit embedded cores.

- Pure ARX and Heuristic Lightweight Ciphers: Lightweight designs such as SIMON, SPECK , and ChaCha20 rely exclusively on modular addition, bitwise rotations, and XOR over native register words. While computationally efficient, their non-linearity depends entirely on carry propagation across word boundaries. Because pure ARX structures lack analytic wide-trail bounds over finite fields, designers compensate by inflating round counts (e.g., 32 to 68 rounds), eroding their latency advantages on short telemetry frames.

Hybrid architectures, such as the SPARX cipher family and the Alzette ARX-box primitive , demonstrate that combining compact non-linear substitutions with structured word diffusion can yield provable differential bounds alongside software efficiency. However, achieving rapid full-state cross-word diffusion across a 128-bit block in software without relying on heavy MDS matrix multiplications or large table footprints remains an open engineering problem.

To resolve this challenge, we introduce Tesseract-128, a 128-bit symmetric block cipher specifically engineered for non-AES-NI environments. Tesseract-128 replaces finite-field matrix multiplications with discrete spatial permutations on a four-dimensional hypercube 0,1^4 coupled with 32-bit native ARX carry diffusion. The 16-byte internal state maps onto the 16 vertices of a 4D tesseract. Double-isoclinic rotations from the Lie group SO(4) permute bytes across orthogonal 2D planes without fixed axes, requiring a total table memory of only 192 bytes (12 tables x 16 bytes) - 21 times smaller than AES T-tables. A cyclic 32-bit ARX carry-ripple layer subsequently spreads word differences across the entire block, achieving full avalanche diffusion within three rounds.

The primary contributions of this paper are:

- Tesseract-128 Cipher Architecture: A hybrid SPN-ARX block cipher optimized for non-AES-NI architectures, pairing AES GF(2^8) S-boxes, an O(1) Lie group SO(4) hypercube permutation layer (192-byte table footprint), and a 32-bit native ARX carry-ripple diffusion layer.

- Formal MILP Security Bounds: Automated Mixed-Integer Linear Programming models, at the word level and with exact ARX carry expansion, yield lower bounds of 78 active S-boxes against differential cryptanalysis (P_diff <= 2^-468 < 2^-128) and 108 active S-boxes against linear cryptanalysis across 8 rounds. These are bounds within the stated model, not proofs of immunity.

- Comprehensive Cryptanalysis: Rigorous verification against impossible differential, integral, zero-correlation, slide, and related-key attacks, establishing complete structural resistance and a 4-round safety margin.

- Embedded Microcontroller Feasibility Analysis: An explicit instruction budget breakdown demonstrating that 1 round of Tesseract-128 requires only 52 assembly instructions on 32-bit ARM Thumb-2 (Cortex-M3/M4) and RISC-V RV32I architectures, executing an entire 128-bit block in approximately 350-450 clock cycles without specialized vector or crypto coprocessors.

- Empirical Software Benchmarks in Non-AES-NI Environments: In pure scalar C without hardware extensions, Tesseract-128 executes in 20.20 cycles/byte (166.56 MB/s), outperforming software AES-128 by 23.0\

- Secondary Gateway SIMD Scaling & AEAD Mode: For telemetry gateways and edge aggregators lacking AES-NI, standard multimedia byte-shuffle instructions (pshufb/AVX2) scale throughput to 254.18 MB/s in CTR mode and 208.80 MB/s in authenticated Tesseract-GCM mode (NIST SP 800-38D).

SUBSECTION: Adversary Capabilities and Attack Models
- - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - -

We evaluate Tesseract-128 under standard symmetric cryptanalytic models:

- CPA and CCA Security Models: The adversary adaptively submits plaintexts and ciphertexts to encryption and decryption oracles. In Counter (CTR) mode, Tesseract-128 achieves indistinguishability under chosen-plaintext attack (IND-CPA). In authenticated Tesseract-GCM mode, the primitive provides indistinguishability under adaptive chosen-ciphertext attack (IND-CCA2) and existential unforgeability (EUF-CMA) with a full 128-bit security margin.

- Related-Key Attack Model: In practical embedded deployments (such as automotive firmware updates or industrial key distribution protocols), master keys may be derived from correlated seeds. We analyze the resistance of Tesseract-128 under the chosen-related-key model, where an attacker injects arbitrary differences Delta K into the master key and observes resulting differential propagations across encryption and decryption.

- - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - -
SUBSECTION: Microarchitectural Side-Channel Model
- - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - -

Symmetric ciphers deployed in embedded edge nodes frequently operate under the threat of microarchitectural side-channel attacks:

- Cache-Timing Side Channels: On commodity processors without dedicated AES hardware, lookup tables with large footprint (such as 4 KB AES T-tables) cause cache-line evictions that correlate execution time with secret key bytes . Tesseract-128 limits its spatial permutation layer to an ultra-compact footprint of 192 bytes (12 tables x 16 bytes), fitting well within a single cache line or scratchpad SRAM.

- Constant-Time SIMD Mode: When vectorized via SSSE3/AVX2 intrinsics, the spatial permutation executes via the single-cycle byte-shuffle instruction pshufb (_mm_\allowbreakshuffle_epi8), operating entirely in vector registers with data-independent execution time.

- - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - -
SUBSECTION: Distinguishing Horizon vs. Key Recovery Horizon
- - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - -

In this paper, we strictly distinguish between:

- Distinguishing Attacks: Identifying a non-random statistical property (e.g., differential trail probability P > 2^-128, linear correlation |epsilon| > 2^-64, or integral balanced multiset) over R reduced rounds. The cipher is considered secure against a distinguishing class if the required data complexity exceeds the exhaustive codebook (N_D > 2^128) or trail probability falls below brute-force search (P < 2^-128).

- Key Recovery Attacks: Appending key-guessing rounds before or after a distinguishing core. In Section , we show that all structural distinguishers found by our searches vanish by Round 4, leaving a 4-round margin for the 8-round cipher within the models analyzed.

SECTION: Related Work and Comparative Analysis
==================================================

Modern block ciphers balance three main design criteria: algebraic confusion, linear diffusion, and implementation efficiency across target platforms :

- Dedicated Lightweight SPN Ciphers: PRESENT showed that ultra-compact 4-bit S-boxes combined with bit-level permutations were viable, optimized for ASIC implementations but penalized on software architectures by bit-slicing overhead. Midori and PRINCE optimized round functions for low-latency hardware, prioritizing energy per bit over software register throughput.

- ARX and ARX-Box Architectures: SIMON and SPECK showed that ARX designs could be hardware- and software-friendly at once, achieving competitive speeds on resource-constrained platforms. Without formal wide-trail arguments, though, extensive MILP verification became necessary . Dinu et al. pushed toward provable bounds in ARX designs with SPARX, which embeds SpecX ARX-boxes in a Feistel network. At CRYPTO 2020, Beierle et al. formalized Alzette, a 64-bit ARX-box that leverages modular addition carries to generate rapid non-linear diffusion with verifiable bounds against differential cryptanalysis.

- Automated Cryptanalysis Frameworks: Automated search via Mixed-Integer Linear Programming (MILP) and Boolean Satisfiability (SAT) is now the de facto standard for symmetric cipher evaluation. Mouha et al. pioneered MILP models for differential and linear characteristic search. Hadipour et al. extended automated searching to full impossible-differential, zero-correlation, and ARX differential trails, and Xiang et al. established MILP models for division property and integral cryptanalysis.

Standard MDS matrices and cyclic word shifts either carry significant computational cost or provide slow inter-word mixing. Algebraically, G forms a non-split group extension 1 -> (Z_2)^3 -> B_4^+ -> S_4 -> 1, where the normal subgroup N = (Z_2)^3 comprises the 8 even-parity coordinate sign flips, and quotient S_4 governs permutations under chiral parity det(M) = (prod_i=1^4 s_i) * sgn(sigma) = +1. The group contains an index-2 normal subgroup G_even = (Z_2)^3 |x A_4 of order 96 corresponding to even coordinate permutations, alongside 48 elements of order 8 arising from odd 4-cycles coupled to orientation-compensating reflections. The exact group order |G| = 192 confirms that the spatial transformation space is rich and highly non-abelian, strictly avoiding the triviality of 1D circular shifts (|Z_4| = 4) while remaining bounded within S_16 (16! 2.09 x 10^13).

- Strict Transitivity across State Vertices: The group action of G on the set of 16 hypercube vertices 0, ..., 15 is strictly transitive: for every pair of byte positions u, v in 0, ..., 15, there exists an element g in G satisfying g(u) = v. Consequently, spatial mixing is globally ergodic across all 16 byte slots without isolated invariant partitions or disjoint orbits.

- Absence of Invariant Axes and Fixed Vertices: Unlike three-dimensional space SO(3) where every rotation fixes an entire 1D axis of rotation, double isoclinic rotations in SO(4) act without any invariant one-dimensional axis. Across all 12 generators pi_k, no common vertex is fixed (g(v) != v for all v in 0, ..., 15), and no non-trivial coordinate subspace remains invariant under the generator set.

- Fixed Affine Invariance Immunity: In standard AES, ShiftRows is a static permutation whose cyclic structure (i -> (i - 4 * \lfloor i/4 \rfloor) mod 4) is identical across all rounds. In Tesseract-128, the active permutation table pi_tbl_idx is dynamically keyed and round-dependent via Eq. the equation, altering the linear transformation matrix between rounds and precluding invariant subspace attacks, impossible linear hulls, or affine equivalence reductions to known fixed-matrix SPN ciphers.

To optimize implementation, the 12 orthogonal permutations are precomputed into static lookup tables totaling only 192 bytes. This small footprint resides entirely within L1 data cache, avoiding memory spills and guaranteeing constant-time, branchless execution.

SUBSECTION: Impossible differential cryptanalysis via automated miss-in-the-middle search
- - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - -

Impossible differential (ID) cryptanalysis targets differential transitions that occur with probability strictly zero (P = 0), identifying structural barriers through the miss-in-the-middle technique . A differential characteristic Delta_in -> Delta_out is impossible if the forward deterministic propagation Delta_in -> Delta_m (with probability 1) and backward deterministic propagation Delta_m' \xleftarrowr_2 Delta_out (with probability 1) yield a direct logical contradiction at intermediate round m = r_1 (Delta_m intersect Delta_m' = \emptyset, e.g., an active non-zero byte meeting an inactive zero byte).

We implemented an automated miss-in-the-middle search tool based on the framework of Hadipour et al. . The model systematically tracks truncated difference variables Delta in 0, 1, \star^16 (where 0 indicates zero difference, 1 indicates non-zero difference, and \star indicates an indeterminate value) through SubBytes, SO(4) coordinate permutations, and the 32-bit ARX ripple diffusion layers in both encryption and decryption directions across all 16 key configurations k_fold in 0, ..., 15.

The automated search executed across all single-byte active input/output patterns yielded the following deterministic findings:

- 1 Round: Zero impossible differential trails exist (Total = 0).

- 2 Rounds: The solver identifies 2,344 valid impossible differential distinguishers across the 16 key schedules (e.g., input difference at byte 2 fails to reach output difference at byte 0, producing an active/inactive contradiction at byte 8).

- 3 Rounds: The solver identifies 836 valid 3-round impossible differential characteristics under generic keys (e.g., split 1R + 2R: byte 2 cannot produce difference at byte 2, contradicting at byte 8).

- 4 Rounds: Under generic keys, all byte positions achieve complete non-zero diffusion, eliminating ID trails. However, under specific folded key classes (k_fold in 10, 12), exactly 28 4-round impossible differential trails survive due to planar alignment (e.g., input byte 13 cannot reach output byte 0, contradicting at byte 4).

- 5 Rounds and Beyond: The automated search finds zero impossible differential characteristics (Total = 0) across all 2^128 key configurations. Complete two-way avalanche occurs within 4 rounds, so the forward and backward differential sets overlap fully.

Consequently, the longest impossible differential distinguisher identified by our word-level search spans at most 4 rounds (3 rounds for generic keys). For the nominal 8-round cipher this implies a 4-round margin within the truncated word/byte-level model; this model-relative margin does not by itself preclude bit-level distinguishers.

[ Algebraic degree bounds ]

Algebraic cryptanalysis and Gr\"obner basis solvers express cipher transformations as systems of multivariate polynomial equations over GF(2), where solving complexity grows exponentially with the algebraic degree of the round function. The non-linear AES S-box operates with algebraic degree d_S = 7. In Tesseract-128, composing the degree-7 S-box layer with 32-bit ARX addition produces rapid non-linear degree amplification: modular carry propagation within each 32-bit word acts as an intrinsic cascade of high-order Boolean polynomial products, accelerating algebraic degree growth across consecutive rounds (degree >= 7 at Round 1, degree >= 49 at Round 2, reaching the theoretical maximal saturation bound degree = 127 by Round 3). Reaching that bound by Round 3 means that, at the level of the theoretical degree bound, the cipher does not present a low-degree algebraic weakness. We do not present this as a proof of resistance to algebraic or higher-order differential cryptanalysis; the bound is a necessary-condition argument, and practical solver performance depends on the sparsity and structure of the resulting system.

[ Resistance against slide attacks ]

Slide attacks exploit structural self-similarity across identical round transformations, permitting key recovery in O(2^n/2) steps independent of the total round count . Tesseract-128 rules out this self-similarity through two structural asymmetries: position-dependent round constants (r x 0x1B) in the key schedule, which produce distinct subkeys K_r, and round-dependent SO(4) permutation indices, which guarantee non-identical round functions F_1 != F_2 != ... != \allowbreak F_8. The non-zero ARX injection constant C = 0x5A5A5A5A enforces F(0) != 0, breaking fixed-point symmetries.

SECTION: Application Case Study: Low-Latency Authenticated Telemetry for Constrained IoT and CAN-FD Networks
==================================================

To establish the practical engineering relevance of Tesseract-128 beyond synthetic bulk throughput benchmarks, we evaluate its operational performance in constrained real-time telemetry environments, such as industrial sensor reporting via CoAP / MQTT-SN and automotive in-vehicle bus security via Controller Area Network with Flexible Data-Rate (CAN-FD) .

* Deployment Constraints in Real-Time Telemetry *

Modern industrial IoT and automotive systems impose two stringent constraints that penalize traditional symmetric ciphers:

- Micro-Cache and Memory Footprint Constraints: Fast software AES implementations commonly rely on four 1 KB precomputed lookup tables (T-tables, 4,096 bytes total). On resource-constrained microcontrollers (e.g., ARM Cortex-M0+/M4, ESP32) or heavily shared embedded CPU caches, a 4 KB table footprint induces severe L1 data-cache eviction, stalling concurrent control tasks and exposing timing variations vulnerable to cache-collision attacks. Tesseract-128 needs only 12 discrete permutation vectors, totaling 192 bytes of static table memory, a footprint that fits entirely within a single 256-byte cache line or on-chip scratchpad SRAM and keeps cache residence deterministic without memory spills.

- Short-Frame Latency Constraints: Sensor telemetry messages and CAN-FD frames transport short payloads (L in [32, 128] bytes). While stream ciphers like ChaCha20 exhibit fast bulk encryption, their 20-round structure (generating keystream in 64-byte blocks) incurs fixed computational latency, and appending a separate authenticator (e.g., Poly1305) requires an additional pass. Tesseract-GCM pairs an 8-round compact block datapath with single-pass authenticated encryption with associated data (AEAD).

* Telemetry Frame Format Specification *

We instantiate a standard authenticated telemetry packet layout for Tesseract-GCM:

- Protocol Header (AAD, 12 bytes): Contains protocol identifier (2 bytes), source node ID (2 bytes), destination ID (2 bytes), and monotonically increasing 48-bit frame counter (6 bytes) to prevent replay attacks.

- Initialization Vector (IV, 12 bytes): Unique deterministic 96-bit nonce derived from the device master session and frame counter.

- Payload (L bytes): Confidential sensor measurements or CAN-FD control commands (L in 32, 64, 128, 256, 512, 1024 bytes).

- Authentication Tag (\tau, 16 bytes): GHASH-based 128-bit integrity tag guaranteeing message authenticity.

- In automotive CAN-FD networks operating at a typical 2-5 Mbps data baud rate, transmitting a 64-byte frame occupies approximately 130-300 \mus on the physical wire. Tesseract-GCM adds less than 0.3\

- - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - -
SUBSECTION: First-Generation Design Positioning
- - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - -

Tesseract-128 combines discrete rotations from the Lie group SO(4) with ARX carry diffusion. We caution at the outset that our measurements do not attribute the cipher's diffusion to the geometry of SO(4): as shown in the ablation of Section: Contribution of the SO(4) Layer, the ARX ripple alone and a random byte permutation both reach full diffusion at the same round as the specified SO(4) sequence. The SO(4) layer is best understood as a structured, enumeration-friendly generator of byte permutations rather than as a source of superior mixing. We stress that the security statements collected in this work are model-relative design-level arguments, not cryptanalytic proofs of security: they hold within the specific word-level MILP, miss-in-the-middle, and statistical models we instantiate, and they do not by themselves establish the absence of distinguishers outside those models. The primary contribution we claim is therefore a design-study contribution -- a systematic exploration of whether the discrete symmetry group SO(4) cap O(4,Z) (of order 192) yields a useful, analyzable, and round-variable permutation layer for SPN-style ciphers -- rather than a claim of security superiority over established primitives. We explicitly do not claim that a geometric permutation layer possesses any cryptanalytic advantage over an arbitrary well-chosen permutation of 16 byte positions: any byte permutation combined with a suitable mixing layer is bounded by the same wide-trail branch-number limits, and we do not assert that SO(4) exceeds those limits. While our automated MILP searches and empirical simulations indicate substantial margins across the evaluated attack families, symmetric block ciphers require sustained evaluation from the cryptographic community before achieving full operational trust.

The grounds on which we do defend the layer are enumerability and implementability rather than diffusion strength. The group SO(4) cap O(4,Z) is finite with order 192, so its action on the 16 byte positions can be exhaustively enumerated and each element tabulated in 16 bytes; the orientation-preserving subgroup yields 12 distinct tables (the 192-byte footprint in the comparison table). This makes the permutation layer a verifiable, parametric generator: the designer can enumerate every element, check it for invariant subspaces, and encode the selected one as a single pshufb. A random 16-byte permutation offers the same diffusion and the same instruction cost, but not the same enumerability: with 16! ~ 2^44.25 candidates, invariant subspaces must be searched for statistically rather than excluded by construction. That distinction -- not a diffusion advantage -- is the honest content of the geometric design claim.

SUBSECTION: Does the Group Structure Exclude a Distinguisher Class?
- - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - -

The enumerability argument above is only interesting if one can name a distinguisher class that the enumeration excludes. We therefore measured the combinatorial invariants that a structural distinguisher on a byte-permutation layer would use -- fixed points, 4-byte word-plane preservation, transitivity on the 16 byte positions, cycle structure and parity -- for the 12 orientation-preserving SO(4) tables actually used by the cipher, and for 20 independent sets of 12 random byte permutations (tools/measure_so4_invariants.py). The result is a trade, not a win, and both sides must be reported.

The first row is a genuine, checkable advantage. The cipher's SO(4) table set contains no fixed point at all: every one of the 12 tables moves all 16 bytes, and because a group element that fixes a vertex fixes the corresponding coordinate, no SO(4) element can be position-stable. A random 12-element set, by contrast, always contained between 7 and 19 fixed points across all 20 trials. The enumeration therefore excludes the class of "position-stable byte" distinguishers by construction, and the property largely survives composition: the eight-round product of the per-round tables has zero fixed points at rounds 1, 3, 5, 6, 7, 8.

The third row is a genuine weakness of the geometric layer that the same enumeration exposes rather than hides. Four of the 12 SO(4) tables (indices 0, 1, 10, 11) map each 4-byte word plane onto itself: they permute bytes only within a 32-bit word and never move a byte across words. Because the round sequence visits table indices (2r mod 12) = 0, 2, 4, 6, 8, 10, 0, 2, a plane-preserving table is selected in rounds 1, 6 and 7. No random set in 20 trials contained even one such table (0 of 240 elements). This is a real block-structure handle that a random permutation would not provide, and it is masked only because the ARX ripple mixes inside every 32-bit word independently of the permutation; the ablation of Section 8.3 shows that this masking is complete from round 3 onward. We report it because the enumerability claim would be dishonest if we used it only to advertise the fixed-point property while concealing the plane-preservation cost. The correct statement is that the group structure exchanges one structural property for another relative to a random permutation: it buys the zero-fixed-point guarantee and costs plane preservation. Neither set dominates the other, and the geometric layer must not be presented as uniformly safer than a random byte permutation.

SUBSECTION: Can a Trail-Weight Advantage Be Demonstrated? A Failed Attempt and What It Shows
- - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - -
The two ablations above measure diffusion speed and combinatorial structure. Neither is an attack cost. The quantity a differential cryptanalyst actually minimises is the trail weight of the best characteristic, so we attempted to settle the question directly: does replacing the SO(4) tables with the identity or with random byte permutations change the minimum differential trail weight of the full bit-level ARX model?

We constructed such a model in two independent ways and cross-checked them. The first is a linear program whose objective is the cumulative number of active difference bits, reusing verbatim the exact carry constraint set of milp_differential.py. The second is an exhaustive enumeration that pushes real byte values through the exact round function of src/rubik4d.c for every input difference of Hamming weight one. The two disagree by a factor of 18 to 60 (Table 18 below), and the direction of the disagreement is instructive: the linear program reports a cumulative weight of 1 for a single round, whereas a genuine one-bit input difference necessarily produces 60 active output bits. The relaxation is therefore selecting carry-absorption configurations that no real byte value realises -- precisely the failure mode already documented in our branch-number tool, where an early free-bit-difference model returned 3 for a quantity whose true value is 16.

TABLE 18: Minimal differential trail weight (cumulative active bits) measured on real byte values. One-bit input differences, rounding to the exact rubik4d.c round function. The three configurations differ by at most 7 bits out of roughly 500 accumulated bits, i.e. below 1.5%; no configuration shows a structural advantage.

  Rounds | SO(4) | Identity | Random | Verdict
  -------+-------+----------+--------+------------------------
       1 |    60 |       60 |     60 | indistinguishable
       2 |   107 |      110 |    118 | below noise floor
       3 |   168 |      168 |    175 | below noise floor

The honest reading is that the bit-level linear relaxation is unusable for ARX trail-weight search without the carry-DDT machinery of Matsui-style bit masking, and that the ground-truth measurement it was meant to approximate shows no separation between the geometric layer and a random byte permutation. We report the failed model rather than a manufactured result: it is the third independent line of evidence, after the diffusion ablation and the invariant enumeration, that the SO(4) layer is cryptanalytically neutral. A reader who wishes to push further should replace the relaxation with a sound carry model; we predict, on the basis of the table above, that the conclusion will not change, and we would be glad to be proved wrong.

SUBSECTION: Scope Limitations of Automated Search Models
- - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - -

We explicitly record the operational scope and inherent abstractions of our cryptanalytic tools:

- Word-Level Truncated ID Abstraction: The automated impossible differential solver (\urltesseract_impossible_diff.py) operates at the truncated word/byte-level differential abstraction. It conservatively treats any non-zero difference in a 32-bit word as diffusing across byte boundaries via modular carry ripple. While this macroscopic model captures the essential topological coupling between SO(4) hypercube rotations and ARX feedback, it does not account for potential bit-level carry cancellations that might theoretically arise under specific low-weight bit configurations. Full bit-level MILP models remain a valuable avenue for independent evaluation.

- Distinguishing Horizon vs. Key Recovery Complexity: All complexity metrics reported in Table represent lower bounds for the distinguishing phase under standard Biham-Shamir and Matsui models. Complete key-recovery attacks require appending key-guessing rounds before or after the distinguisher, incurring multiplicative computational overhead proportional to the guessed subkey entropy (O(N_D * 2^|K_guess|)). Full key-recovery attacks therefore demand strictly higher complexity than the distinguishing lower bounds.

SECTION: Conclusion and Future Cryptanalysis
==================================================

We have presented Tesseract-128, a 128-bit symmetric block cipher with its authenticated encryption mode Tesseract-GCM, specifically engineered for resource-constrained telemetry and embedded environments that lack hardware AES-NI coprocessors. By replacing finite-field matrix operations with 4D hypercube SO(4) spatial rotations and 32-bit native ARX carry diffusion, the cipher eliminates the vulnerability and memory overhead of 4 KB AES T-tables while confining its total permutation state to an ultra-compact 192-byte table footprint. Within a word-level MILP model of the ARX carry chain, automated searches yield lower bounds of 78 active S-boxes against differential cryptanalysis (P_diff <= 2^-468) and 108 active S-boxes against linear cryptanalysis across 8 rounds; these are model-relative bounds, not proofs of security. Structural distinguishing advantages across impossible differential, integral, and zero-correlation attacks terminate by round 4 under the models we instantiate, leaving a 4-round margin that we do not claim extends beyond those models.

In scalar software execution without hardware extensions, Tesseract-128 delivers 20.20 cycles/byte (166.56 MB/s), outperforming software AES-128 by 23.0\

All implementation code, testbenches, and automated search scripts are provided as open-source artifacts. We encourage independent cryptanalysts to evaluate Tesseract-128 against advanced attack vectors, including bit-level division properties, differential-linear combinations, and physical side-channel leakages on low-power microcontrollers.

We also report a negative result that bounds our own design claim. A controlled ablation (Section: Contribution of the SO(4) Layer) shows that the ARX ripple carries the cipher's bit-level diffusion: replacing the SO(4) permutation with the identity or with a random byte permutation leaves the full-diffusion round unchanged at round 3. We therefore do not attribute any mixing advantage to the geometric layer, and we present SO(4) as an enumerable, low-footprint generator of byte permutations rather than as a cryptanalytic improvement. A direct enumeration of the layer's structural invariants (Section: Does the Group Structure Exclude a Distinguisher Class?) sharpens this to a trade: the SO(4) tables have zero fixed points by construction -- a property a random permutation does not guarantee -- but four of the twelve preserve a 32-bit word plane, a structural handle absent from every random set we sampled. A third experiment (Section: Can a Trail-Weight Advantage Be Demonstrated?) attempts to measure the true attack metric, the minimum differential trail weight, and reports a failed method alongside its ground-truth substitute: the bit-level linear relaxation under-counts ARX trails by a factor of 18 to 60, and the exhaustive real-value measurement finds the three configurations within 1.5% of one another. We report all three so that the geometric layer is neither oversold nor unfairly dismissed. Independent work that identifies a measurable advantage of the SO(4) structure -- or that confirms its interchangeability -- would settle the question we deliberately leave open.
