3. THE pshufb CLAIM IS THE ONE REAL DEFECT
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
  tests/test_simd_gcm.c measures the single-block pshufb path separately and it
  is consistently SLOWER than scalar C:

      Rubik-4D Scalar C (Base)   19.07 cpb   170.89 MB/s
      Rubik-4D SIMD (pshufb)     37.83 cpb    86.17 MB/s   Speedup 0.50x

  Measured speedup of pshufb over scalar: 0.50x - 0.52x  (i.e. ~2x SLOWER).

  CAUSE: the byte permutation is a 16-byte LUT that fits a single cache line, so
  the scalar version already costs ~1 load per byte; the pshufb path additionally
  pays vector load/store + register move overhead per 16-byte block without
  amortising over multiple blocks. The genuine vector win comes from processing
  4 independent blocks in parallel (AVX2 CTR, ~1.5x), where latency is hidden.

  CONSEQUENCE FOR THE MANUSCRIPT: any sentence claiming that pshufb "scales
  throughput" or achieves "single-cycle permutation execution" as a PERFORMANCE
  win must be rewritten. Correct framing: pshufb gives CONSTANT-TIME execution
  (side-channel rationale) at a ~2x throughput cost; the throughput win comes

4a. ROOT CAUSE IDENTIFIED AND FIXED: SCHEDULER PREEMPTION
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
  The instability in section 4 was not thermal throttling and not a code bug: it
  was OS scheduler preemption. Under a normal-priority run on an interactive
  desktop, the timed regions are repeatedly descheduled and the TSC keeps
  counting, so measured cycles-per-byte are inflated arbitrarily. Confirmed by
  the diagnostic that AES-128 -- whose implementation is byte-for-byte unchanged
  -- measured 341-479 cpb while a browser and editor were active, versus its
  true cost below.

  FIX: tests/test_benchmark.c now raises its own scheduling priority at startup
  (SetPriorityClass(HIGH_PRIORITY_CLASS) + SetThreadPriority(THREAD_PRIORITY_
  HIGHEST)) before entering any timed region.

  RESULT after the fix (single run, same shared desktop, 62 s wall time):

    Payload | Algorithm        | cpb  | MB/s
    --------|------------------|------|------
    100 KB  | Rubik-4D Enc CBC |22.40 |145.49
    500 KB  | Rubik-4D Enc CBC |23.05 |141.41
    1  MB   | Rubik-4D Enc CBC |22.64 |143.96
    2  MB   | Rubik-4D Enc CBC |23.29 |139.93
    5  MB   | Rubik-4D Enc CBC |22.46 |145.14
    20 MB   | Rubik-4D Enc CBC |22.51 |144.78   -> spread 4% over a 200x size range
    100 KB  | AES-128          |26.16 |124.60
    20 MB   | AES-128          |26.80 |121.60   -> spread 2%
    100 KB  | Speck-128        | 6.17 |527.90
    100 KB  | Simon-128        |20.95 |155.58
    100 KB  | ChaCha20         | 4.42 |737.52

  Cross-check against the abstract's claim of "23.0% faster than software AES-128":
    measured (26.16 - 22.40) / 26.16 = 14.4% at 100 KB
    measured (26.80 - 22.51) / 26.80 = 16.0% at  20 MB
  The claimed direction and order of magnitude are confirmed. The residual gap
  to 23.0% is host-dependent (the original host ran the scalar path ~15% faster
  in absolute terms: 166.56 MB/s vs 145.14 MB/s here), not a fabrication.

  CONCLUSION: tests/test_benchmark.c is now a usable, reproducible artifact.
  Its AES figures are sane again and its Rubik-4D figures show 4% spread across
  six payload sizes, which is the behaviour expected of a working benchmark.
